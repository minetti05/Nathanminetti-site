In the summer of 2020, I picked up an instant camera for the first time, with no real plan beyond wanting to slow down and look closer at the neighborhood I'd suddenly stopped leaving. Brooklyn had gone quiet in a way I'd never seen it, the usual noise of the borough replaced by something closer to stillness. I started photographing what was actually in front of me, day by day, while refining technique and discovering intention beyond curiosity.

Then, the quiet broke. Murals appeared on shuttered storefronts and roll-down gates across the neighborhood, painted by hands that turned closed businesses into public statements. Coney Island sat empty against hard blue skies, its rides motionless, its boardwalk given over to gulls and a handful of people finding their own way to be outside. Somebody built a snowman with a cinnamon stick for a nose. A pizza party for the socially distanced. None of it was staged. All of it, looking back, was a record of a place and its people improvising a way through something none of us had a script for.

This body of work is not really about the pandemic. It's about Brooklyn learning how to hold its breath and then breathe anew, and about me learning — frame by frame, instant by instant — how to see. The instant film format felt right for that: each photograph is singular, undoable, exactly as good or as flawed as the moment allowed. There was no reshoot. That felt honest to what those months were.
